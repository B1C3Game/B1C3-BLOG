Cloud is Configuration, Not Location

Cloud is an operating model. Sovereignty is a control requirement. Products decide which combinations are actually deployable.

Published: 2026-06-11 · Updated: 2026-08-25

When a Swedish government agency discusses moving Teams, Confluence, or critical infrastructure to the cloud, one conversation often fractures into three.

The lawyers ask: Who can legally reach the data?

The provisioning engineers say: We can put a server anywhere.

The decision-makers ask: What is the practical difference between cloud and the servers we already have?

These are all valid questions. They are not the same question.

The confusion starts when "cloud" becomes shorthand for location, outsourcing, software as a service, American ownership, or somebody else's data center. Those things can overlap. None of them defines cloud by itself.

Cloud is an operating model. Sovereignty is a control requirement. A product is a set of deployment choices. If we collapse all three into one word, we can comply on paper and still build the wrong thing.

What "Cloud" Actually Means

The durable definition comes from NIST SP 800-145. It describes cloud computing through five essential characteristics:

  • on-demand self-service;
  • broad network access;
  • resource pooling;
  • rapid elasticity;
  • measured service.

NIST also recognizes public, private, community, and hybrid cloud deployment models.

That matters because cloud is not defined by whose building contains the hardware. A private platform in a government-operated data center can have cloud characteristics. A rented virtual machine that still needs a manual ticket for every change may have fewer of them.

Some traits commonly associated with public SaaS, such as continuous vendor-controlled updates or global distribution, are not required by the definition. They are product and operating choices.

The more useful question is therefore not:

Is it in the cloud or on-premises?

It is:

Which operating characteristics and control boundaries does this workload require, and which deployable service can provide them?

Location Is Only One Control

A Swedish cloud region can answer an important question: where is a particular service designed to store or process particular data?

It does not answer every other question:

  • Which company and jurisdiction govern the provider?
  • Can support staff outside Sweden or the EU access the service?
  • Who controls the encryption keys?
  • Where do identity, telemetry, backup, and billing dependencies live?
  • Can the agency continue during a provider or network outage?
  • Can the service be moved without rebuilding the organization around a new product?
  • Who can recover the service, and has recovery been tested?

AWS has a Stockholm region. Azure lists Sweden Central and Sweden South. That gives Swedish organizations more location options, but a region is not a complete sovereignty claim. Residency, jurisdiction, operational control, continuity, and exit are separate properties.

Sovereignty is therefore better treated as a control profile than as a map pin.

Sweden Now Has a Cloud Policy

An earlier version of this article argued that Sweden lacked a published strategy. That became false on 29 May 2026, when the government published En molnpolicy för Sverige, its first cloud policy for public administration.

The policy is more pragmatic than either "cloud first" or "keep everything Swedish."

It says each public actor remains responsible for its own assessment. Choices should be risk-based and reflect the workload's requirements for function, security, control (rådighet), sovereignty, robustness, and cost-effectiveness. It also says public administration can continue to use market-leading products from companies outside the EU where those requirements permit it.

That is a better framing than a universal rule. Information and services do not all carry the same legal, security, or continuity requirements.

But policy is not yet a deployable service catalogue. An implementation team still needs concrete answers:

  • Which information classes may use which service models?
  • Which products have been assessed, under which configurations and contracts?
  • What evidence is required for support access, recovery, and exit?
  • Which shared state-operated services are available now?
  • Who owns the decision when legal, security, architecture, and procurement assessments disagree?

The strategy exists. The remaining gap is translating principles into repeatable decisions and usable platforms.

The Legal Question Is Conditional

It is too broad to say that American cloud providers are prohibited because US law conflicts with Swedish law.

The real analysis is conditional.

The Swedish Authority for Privacy Protection, IMY, explains that personal-data transfers outside the EU/EEA are permitted under defined conditions. Depending on the recipient and transfer, those can include an adequacy decision, participation in the EU-US Data Privacy Framework, Standard Contractual Clauses, Binding Corporate Rules, and supplementary safeguards.

The European Data Protection Board also makes clear that these transfer mechanisms do not replace ordinary GDPR duties. Organizations still need a legal basis, data minimization, security, processor agreements where applicable, and in some cases a transfer impact assessment.

Provider jurisdiction still matters. Sweden's cloud policy notes that companies in some countries may, under certain conditions, be required to disclose data to national authorities even when the data is stored elsewhere.

The defensible conclusion is not "US cloud is illegal" or "a Swedish region solves it." It is:

Map the actual transfers and access paths, identify the legal mechanism, assess the information's protection value, and decide whether the remaining risk is acceptable for that workload.

Products Constrain Architecture

The discussion becomes concrete when a policy meets an actual product.

Atlassian says Confluence Data Center will reach end of life on 28 March 2029. Standard subscriptions and affected Marketplace apps will become read-only. Atlassian says extended maintenance may exist for some customers by exception, but the general direction is clear: Confluence customers need a migration or replacement decision.

Atlassian presents this as a cloud-platform strategy centered on integrated products, AI, security, and faster innovation. We can debate the business incentives, but we should not state an unverified motive as fact. The operational consequence is enough: the generally available self-managed path is closing.

Microsoft Teams presents a different constraint. Microsoft's architecture documentation describes Teams as part of Microsoft 365, built on services including Entra ID, Microsoft 365 Groups, Microsoft Graph, SharePoint, and Exchange Online. Microsoft documents how a tenant administers Teams. It does not offer the Teams service as software an agency can deploy onto its own Kubernetes cluster.

That means "put Teams and Confluence on sovereign Kubernetes" is not an architecture. Teams cannot simply be moved there, and Confluence Data Center has a vendor deadline.

For each product, the real options may be different:

Workload Plausible paths
Confluence Atlassian Cloud, a future qualifying Atlassian deployment model, temporary Data Center operation, or product replacement
Teams Microsoft 365 under an accepted control model, a future qualifying Microsoft offering, or product replacement
Custom applications Public cloud, private cloud, coordinated state IT, colocation, or a hybrid combination

A sovereignty requirement can therefore force a product decision, not merely a hosting decision.

What Private Cloud Actually Requires

Private cloud is possible. Kubernetes is not what makes it cloud, and Kubernetes is not mandatory.

A credible private-cloud service does need capabilities such as:

  • automated and repeatable provisioning;
  • identity, policy, and secrets management;
  • patching and vulnerability management;
  • observability and incident response;
  • capacity and lifecycle management;
  • tested backup, recovery, and continuity procedures;
  • service ownership, support hours, and an on-call model;
  • cost and usage visibility;
  • a maintained service catalogue and supported application patterns.

Kubernetes may be the right substrate for containerized custom applications. It may be the wrong substrate for a commercial SaaS product that is not available for customer deployment. Virtualization, managed platforms, and shared state-operated services can also deliver cloud characteristics.

The staffing and timeline cannot be estimated honestly without scope. A small internal platform with business-hours support is not the same undertaking as a multi-site service for security-sensitive workloads with continuous support.

Instead of asserting "3 to 5 engineers and 18 to 24 months," define the service first:

  1. Which workloads and data classes are in scope?
  2. What availability and recovery objectives apply?
  3. Which capabilities already exist?
  4. Which responsibilities belong to the platform team, application team, supplier, and security function?
  5. What support and on-call coverage is required?

Only then does a staffing number mean anything.

Hybrid Is a Portfolio, Not a Compromise

The practical path is usually hybrid, but not in the sense of putting every product in two places.

Hybrid means choosing per workload:

  • public SaaS where its control model is acceptable;
  • public or sovereign-provider infrastructure where local capacity and contractual controls are sufficient;
  • coordinated state IT or private cloud where stronger operational control is required;
  • replacement where the product cannot meet the requirement in any available deployment model.

The European Commission's June 2026 cloud policy work reinforces this direction. The proposed Cloud and AI Development Act and wider Technological Sovereignty Package aim to expand European capacity, improve interoperability and switching, and prioritize highly secure capacity for critical use cases.

This did not begin in June 2026. AWS, for example, announced its European Sovereign Cloud in October 2023. The dates matter. Provider initiatives and EU policy belong to the same longer movement, but one should not be presented as an immediate reaction to the other.

The EU direction also does not prove that every required sovereign service is available today. Policy pressure can arrive before deployable alternatives.

The Implementation Gap

What I see from the implementation level is not an absence of intelligence or effort. It is a translation problem.

The lawyer asks who can reach the data.

The infrastructure engineer answers where the VM is hosted.

The product owner asks whether Teams or Confluence will still work.

The procurement function asks which agreement can be used.

Each answer can be correct inside its own boundary and still fail to produce a decision.

The missing capability is someone, or some accountable forum, that can turn policy into a control profile, test real products against it, and return an actionable choice with named residual risks.

That is the expertise gap worth fixing.

Not "cloud expertise" in the narrow sense. Translation across law, information security, product architecture, operations, procurement, and continuity.

The Right Question

Stop asking:

Should we go cloud or on-premises?

Start asking:

What must we control for this workload, and which available product and operating model can prove that control?

Sweden does not need one answer for every workload. It needs a repeatable way to reach an answer, evidence that the chosen service behaves as promised, and a credible path out when the promise changes.

Cloud is configuration, not location.

Sovereignty is control, not ownership alone.

And strategy becomes real only when an implementation team can use it.

Brasklapp

I do not know every decision being made inside central government, every agency assessment, or every procurement now underway.

I do know that the public record changed in May 2026: Sweden now has a cloud policy. This article therefore does not claim that no strategy exists. It asks what happens between that high-level policy and the technician, architect, lawyer, or product owner who has to make a specific service work.

The implementation friction described here is an observation from that level. It is not proof that no work is happening elsewhere.

If there is a mature decision model, approved service catalogue, or shared platform that resolves these questions, making it visible to implementation teams is part of the solution.
